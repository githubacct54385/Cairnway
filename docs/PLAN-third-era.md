# Plan: The Third Era (after Constantine, about AD 313 to 451)

A planning document, not page content. Every date, passage reference and book detail below is from the drafter's general
knowledge and is **U** (unverified) until checked, as in `PLAN-second-era.md` and `REVIEW-second-century.md`.
Proposed slug (the era is organised by East and West, not by one theme): `age-of-the-councils` (file `src/content/eras/age-of-the-councils.md`, `order: 3`).

## Decisions (owner, 2026-11-11)

- **End date: AD 451**, the Council of Chalcedon. The era is AD 313 to 451.
- **Structure: East and West**, each with its own notable events, so long as both have them (they do).
- **Only the notable.** The page keeps to events that scholars treat as well attested and important. Minor events and
  contested legends are left out or mentioned only in the "overstated" notes.

## A note on East and West

The East and West split here is about **language and region**, Greek in the East and Latin in the West. It is **not** the
split between Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches. That break (the "Great Schism") is conventionally dated to AD 1054,
and the churches of this era were in communion with each other, though they quarrelled. The splits that do begin in this
era are different ones: the **Church of the East** parted from the rest after Ephesus (431), and the **Oriental Orthodox**
churches (Coptic, Armenian, Syriac and others) parted after Chalcedon (451). The page should say so plainly, so a reader
does not read the later schism back into the fourth century.

## What scholars tend to treat as notable, and what as uncertain (all U)

**Notable and well attested:** the Council of Nicaea and its creed; Athanasius's long conflict; Augustine's
*Confessions* and *City of God*; Jerome's Vulgate; the growth of monasticism; the Councils of Ephesus and Chalcedon.

**Real but debated:** how sincerely Constantine held to the faith, and what changed for the church as a result; what
Arius actually taught (we know it mostly through his opponents); how many bishops came to Nicaea (the traditional number
is 318, which may be symbolic); whether the Theodosian laws made Christianity "the state religion"; how to date the
Vulgate and the two African councils.

**Commonly repeated but overstated:** that Nicaea chose the books of the Bible (it did not; the lists appear in
Athanasius in 367 and in African councils in the 390s); that Nicaea invented the divinity of Christ; that Constantine
"made Christianity the religion of the empire"; that the church decided doctrine by a vote of a single day.
