# Plan: The Eleventh Era (AD 1648 to 1789)

> Drafted 2026-11-12 as the planning document for the eleventh era, after the Reformation era (AD 1517 to 1648). Planning
> only. Every date, passage reference and book detail is from the drafter's general knowledge and is **U** (unverified)
> until checked, unless marked **F** (read in the full text of a named public-domain edition).

Proposed slug: `reason-and-revival` (file `src/content/eras/reason-and-revival.md`, `order: 11`). Title is a suggestion.

## Why this boundary

The Reformation era closes with the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which set out how the German lands would be divided between
Catholics and Protestants. This era begins there and ends at the French Revolution (1789), when the French state took
over the church's property and clergy, and the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) made priests into civil servants.
In between, Europe's churches lived with the settlement of 1648, Catholic and Protestant renewals grew up alongside each
other, and new ideas questioned the authority of revelation itself.

Ending at 1789 gives the site a natural break before a possible twelfth era on 1789 to 1914, the age of revolution,
liberal churches and the missions.

## Decisions (owner, to confirm)

1. **Start: AD 1648** (Peace of Westphalia), following the Reformation era.
2. **End: AD 1789** (the French Revolution). Alternatives: 1773 (suppression of the Jesuits) or 1790 (the Civil
   Constitution of the Clergy). Owner to choose.
3. **Length: 141 years.** Comparable to the Reformation era, and so the page should be organised by theme, as that plan is.
4. **East is in scope in a limited way**: Peter the Great and the Russian Orthodox Church (the patriarchate abolished in
   1721), the Ottoman frontier receding, and the Orthodox response to Western thought. A few rows only.
5. **Organised by theme, not by strict chronology**: confessional renewal, pietism and revival, Enlightenment critique,
   and the state's power over the church.

## Structure of the page

1. **Settlement and its limits (1648 to 1685):** Westphalia's terms, the Peace in practice, and the Catholic and Protestant
   churches of the post-war years.
2. **Renewal in the Catholic church (1640 to 1789):** the Jansenist controversy, the Jesuit missions and their suppression,
   and the Council's legacy in the Catholic reform movements.
3. **Pietism and revival (1670 to 1750):** Spener's *Pia Desideria* (1675), Halle, the Moravians, and the Wesleys.
4. **Reason and religion (1690 to 1789):** Locke, Hume and Kant, deism, and the challenge to miracles.
5. **The state and the church (1700 to 1789):** the Russian Holy Synod, Joseph II's Edict of Toleration (1781), the
   suppression of the Jesuits, and the church's place in the monarchies.
6. **Revolution (1789):** the Declaration of the Rights of Man, the sale of church lands, and the Civil Constitution.
