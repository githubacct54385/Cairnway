# Plan: The Twelfth Era (AD 1789 to 1870)

> Drafted 2026-11-12 as the planning document for the twelfth era, after the eleventh era (AD 1648 to 1789). Planning
> only. Every date, passage reference and book detail is from the drafter's general knowledge and is **U** (unverified)
> until checked, unless marked **F** (read in the full text of a named public-domain edition).

Proposed slug: `revolution-and-restoration` (file `src/content/eras/revolution-and-restoration.md`, `order: 12`). Title is a
suggestion.

## Why this boundary

The eleventh era ends with the French Revolution and the state's seizure of church property. This era begins there and ends
in 1870, the year the First Vatican Council defined papal infallibility (18 July) and Italian troops entered Rome, ending the
Papal States (20 September). Those two events close the old papal state and set the church's position for the next seventy
years. Both are dated **U** and need checking.

## Decisions (owner, to confirm)

1. **Start: AD 1789**, following the eleventh era. This depends on the open question there (1789 or 1790).
2. **End: AD 1870** (Vatican I and the fall of Rome). The alternative, 1848 (the revolutions), would split the Restoration
   from the papal crisis that followed it, so it is not recommended.
3. **Length: 81 years.**
4. **East is in scope in a limited way**: the Greek War of Independence (from 1821), the Russian church under the tsars, the
   Ottoman reforms of the Tanzimat (1839 onwards), and the Bulgarian Exarchate, set up by firman in 1870.
5. **Organised by theme, not by strict chronology**, as the eleventh era is.

## Structure of the page

1. **Revolution and the church's property (1789 to 1801):** the Civil Constitution's effects, dechristianisation, the death of
   Pius VI in French captivity (1799), and the Concordat of 1801.
2. **Napoleon and the church's lands (1801 to 1815):** the end of the Holy Roman Empire (1806), the annexation of the Papal
   States and the arrest of Pius VII (1809), and the Congress of Vienna.
3. **Restoration and its limits (1815 to 1848):** the Holy Alliance, the restored Jesuits (1814), and the Romantic revival of
   faith, with Chateaubriand's *The Genius of Christianity* (1802).
4. **Revival and the Oxford Movement (1820 to 1850):** Keble's Assize Sermon (1833), the *Tracts for the Times*, Newman's
   conversion (1845), and the Second Great Awakening and the Protestant missions.
5. **Liberal and critical answers (1830 to 1870):** Lamennais and the liberal Catholics, Kierkegaard's critique of the state
   church, and the first modern biblical criticism.
6. **Rome and Vatican I (1846 to 1870):** Pius IX, the revolutions of 1848 and his flight from Rome, the Immaculate Conception
   (1854), the *Syllabus of Errors* (1864), and the council.
7. **The East (1821 to 1870):** the Greek state, the Tanzimat, and the Russian church's place in the empire.
