keyPeople:
  - name: "Ignatius of Antioch"
    role: "Bishop, martyr and letter writer"
    summary: "Eusebius says he was taken from Syria to Rome as a prisoner and, on the way through Asia, wrote letters to the churches of Ephesus, Magnesia, Tralles and Rome from Smyrna, and from Troas to the churches of Philadelphia and Smyrna and to Polycarp. In the letter to Rome he writes that from Syria to Rome he fights with wild beasts, bound to ten leopards, which he explains as a band of soldiers. His letters press hard for one bishop in each church, with elders and deacons under him."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 3.36; Ignatius, Romans 5, Smyrnaeans 8"
  - name: "Polycarp"
    role: "Bishop of Smyrna and martyr"
    summary: "Irenaeus, who says he saw Polycarp in his youth, writes that Polycarp was taught by apostles, was appointed bishop of Smyrna by apostles in Asia, and visited Rome in the time of Anicetus. Polycarp's own short letter to the Philippians mentions Ignatius and says he is sending on the letters of Ignatius that he had. The account of his death says that when the Roman governor urged him to curse Christ, he answered that he had served him for eighty-six years."
    source: "Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.3.4; Polycarp, To the Philippians 13; Martyrdom of Polycarp 9"
  - name: "Pliny the Younger"
    role: "Roman governor of Bithynia and writer"
    summary: "A Roman senator who, as governor in northern Asia Minor, wrote to the emperor Trajan to ask how to treat Christians brought before him. His letter and Trajan's reply are the earliest surviving official Roman statement on the question, and Pliny was not a Christian, which is why the pair are so valuable."
    source: "Pliny, Letters 10.96 and 10.97 (numbered XCVII and XCVIII in the Melmoth translation)"
  - name: "Justin Martyr"
    role: "Christian teacher and defender of the faith"
    summary: "Writes an Apology addressed to the emperor Antoninus Pius in which he describes how Christians baptise and meet on Sundays, and he says Marcion is teaching in his own day. Eusebius says he was a philosopher who preached the word in Rome and that a rival, the Cynic Crescens, plotted against him. The surviving record of his trial says he was condemned by Rusticus, the prefect of Rome, for refusing to sacrifice."
    source: "Justin, First Apology 1, 58, 61, 65 to 67; Eusebius, Church History 4.16 (and the translator's note on the Acts of Justin)"
  - name: "Marcion"
    role: "Teacher from Pontus who broke with the church in Rome"
    summary: "Justin says Marcion taught that the creator was not the father of Christ and preached another god. Irenaeus adds that Marcion cut down the Gospel of Luke, removed what it said about the birth of Jesus, and also dismembered the letters of Paul. Irenaeus says he set aside the prophets and the law. Marcion's own short list of Christian writings is often thought to have pushed other Christians to say what theirs was; see Further reading."
    source: "Justin, First Apology 58; Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.27.2, 3.11.7; for his role in the formation of the New Testament, see Further reading"
  - name: "Irenaeus"
    role: "Bishop of Lyons and writer"
    summary: "A native of Asia who grew up hearing Polycarp, and later bishop of Lyons in Gaul. In Against Heresies he argues from the churches founded by the apostles against Marcion, Valentinus and others, and he argues that there must be four Gospels, no more and no fewer. He also wrote to Victor, bishop of Rome, to urge peace in the dispute about Easter."
    source: "Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.3, 3.11.8; Eusebius, Church History 5.24"
  - name: "Celsus"
    role: "Greek philosopher who wrote against Christianity"
    summary: "Known only from the long reply by Origen, who quotes his work The True Word. Celsus charged that Christians formed secret associations against the law, and he sneered that they spread their message through wool-workers, cobblers and fullers who spoke to children and ignorant women in private houses. Origen answers him point by point."
    source: "Origen, Against Celsus 1.1, 3.55"
  - name: "Victor of Rome"
    role: "Bishop of Rome during the Easter dispute"
    summary: "Eusebius says that Victor tried to cut off all the churches of Asia, who kept Easter on the fourteenth day of the Jewish month, as heterodox, and declared them excommunicate. Other bishops, including Irenaeus, rebuked him."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 5.23, 5.24"
  - name: "Tertullian"
    role: "Latin writer in Carthage"
    summary: "A prolific writer whose Apology defends Christians to the Roman authorities. In it he tells them that the more they mow Christians down the more Christians grow, for the blood of Christians is seed. He also wrote a long work against Marcion."
    source: "Tertullian, Apology 50; Against Marcion"
  - name: "Origen"
    role: "Teacher and scholar of Alexandria and Caesarea"
    summary: "Eusebius gives him much of book 6 of his Church History and says he suffered in the persecution under Decius. His Against Celsus is the fullest answer to an outside critic that survives from this period."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 6.39; Origen, Against Celsus"
  - name: "Cyprian"
    role: "Bishop of Carthage"
    summary: "Bishop during the Decian persecution and after it. In On the Unity of the Church he writes that a person cannot have God as Father who does not have the church as mother. Cyprian's own letters show him writing to confessors in prison after the Decian persecution, and later urging his colleagues to prepare their churches for martyrdom when Valerian's order arrived."
    source: "Cyprian, On the Unity of the Church 6, Epistles 10 and 81 (ANF numbering)"
  - name: "Diocletian"
    role: "Roman emperor, 284 to 305"
    summary: "Lactantius says the emperor and his colleague Galerius watched from the palace at Nicomedia while soldiers destroyed the Christian church there, and that the day chosen was the festival of Terminus. This began the persecution later known as the Great Persecution."
    source: "Lactantius, Of the Manner in Which the Persecutors Died 12"
  - name: "Constantine"
    role: "Roman emperor, from 306"
    summary: "Lactantius says he was told in a dream to put the heavenly sign on his soldiers' shields before the battle at the Milvian bridge. Eusebius, writing his Life of Constantine, says the emperor himself told him, and swore, that he saw a cross of light in the sky above the sun with the words Conquer by this."
    source: "Lactantius 44; Eusebius, Life of Constantine 1.28"

    title: "2 Peter is written"
    summary: "The letter claims to be by Simon Peter and speaks of Paul's letters and of the other scriptures. Most scholars date it after Peter's death, to the early or middle second century, which makes it one of the last books of the New Testament.   - date: "c. AD 110 (debated)"
    title: "Ignatius is taken to Rome, and writes seven letters"
    summary: "Ignatius, bishop of Antioch in Syria, was arrested and sent to Rome to face the wild beasts. On the way he wrote letters to Christian communities in Asia and to the church in Rome. He urges each church to keep together under its bishop, and in his letter to Smyrna he writes that wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the catholic church, the earliest surviving use of that phrase. In the Greek it means the universal church, not a particular denomination."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 3.36; Ignatius, Smyrnaeans 8, Romans 5"
  - date: "c. AD 112 (dating debated)"
    title: "Pliny asks Trajan what to do with the Christians"
    summary: "Pliny, governor of Bithynia in northern Asia Minor, writes that he has never been present at the trial of Christians and does not know how far to punish them. He asks those accused whether they are Christians, asks a second and third time with a threat of punishment, and has those who persist punished; Roman citizens he sends to Rome. People who deny it and curse Christ he releases. He reports that Christians meet on a fixed day before dawn, address a prayer to Christ as to a god, and bind themselves by an oath not to steal, commit fraud or adultery, or break their word, and then share a meal. He calls it a contagious superstition. Trajan replies that Pliny has done right, that Christians are not to be hunted out, that anonymous accusations are not to be accepted, and that anyone who denies it and proves it by worshipping the gods is to be pardoned."
    source: "Pliny, Letters 10.96 and 10.97 (XCVII and XCVIII in Melmoth)"
  - date: "c. AD 110 to 140 (debated)"
    title: "Polycarp writes to the church at Philippi"
    summary: "Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, writes a short letter to the Philippians in which he says that the Philippians and Ignatius had asked him to forward their letters to Syria. He sends the letters of Ignatius that he has, and asks for news about Ignatius and those with him."
    source: "Polycarp, To the Philippians 13; Eusebius, Church History 3.36.13 to 15"
  - date: "c. AD 100 to 150 (all dates debated)"
    title: "The Didache, Barnabas and the Shepherd of Hermas circulate"
    summary: "Three writings read by Christians of the time. The Didache is a short manual of Christian life and practice. The letter of Barnabas is a letter that interprets the Jewish scriptures. The Shepherd is a long work of visions, which the Muratorian fragment says was written in Rome in the time of Pius, and which it says ought to be read but cannot be placed among the prophets or the apostles."
    source: "Didache (ANF vol. 7); Epistle of Barnabas (ANF vol. 1); Shepherd of Hermas (ANF vol. 2); Muratorian fragment (ANF vol. 5)"
  - date: "c. AD 144 to 150 (debated)"
    title: "Marcion breaks with the church in Rome and founds his own churches"
    summary: "Justin, writing in the middle of the century, says that Marcion of Pontus is even now teaching men to deny that God is the maker of heaven and earth, and that many have believed him. Irenaeus says Marcion cut the Gospel of Luke and Paul's letters to remove everything that called the Creator the Father of Jesus. Christians who disagreed with him were pushed to say which writings they did accept."
    source: "Justin, First Apology 58; Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.27.2; Muratorian fragment (rejects writings composed for Marcion)"
  - date: "c. AD 150 to 155 (debated)"
    title: "Justin describes baptism and Sunday worship"
    summary: "In his Apology, addressed to the emperor Antoninus Pius, Justin tells the Romans how Christians worship. New believers are washed in water in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. On the day called Sunday everyone gathers in one place; the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read; the president speaks; they pray; bread and wine and water are brought and the people say Amen; and a portion is sent to those who are absent."
    source: "Justin, First Apology 1, 61, 65 to 67"
  - date: "c. AD 155 to 167 (debated)"
    title: "Polycarp is martyred at Smyrna"
    summary: "The letter from the church of Smyrna that records his death is the earliest surviving account of the martyrdom of a Christian other than those in the New Testament. It says he was arrested, and that when the proconsul urged him to curse Christ he answered that he had served him eighty-six years. The letter names the proconsul as Statius Quadratus, and Eusebius places it under Verus. Modern scholars disagree about the year, and some argue that parts of the account were added later."
    source: "Martyrdom of Polycarp 9, 21 (and translator's note); Eusebius, Church History 4.15"
  - date: "c. AD 160 to 168 (debated)"
    title: "Justin is tried and executed in Rome"
    summary: "According to the surviving account of his trial, Justin and six companions were condemned by Rusticus, the prefect of Rome, when they refused to sacrifice to the gods. Eusebius says Justin had been plotted against by the philosopher Crescens. The translator of the edition we use says the date is very hard to determine, and that the tradition placing it under Marcus Aurelius has the most in its favour, since Rusticus became prefect of Rome in AD 163."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 4.16 (translator's note on the Acts of Justin)"
  - date: "c. AD 170 to 180 (debated)"
    title: "Montanus and the new prophecy"
    summary: "A hostile writer quoted by Eusebius says that a recent convert named Montanus first appeared in a village in Mysia, on the border of Phrygia, when Gratus was proconsul of Asia. The writer says the church at Ancyra in Galatia was agitated by what the movement called prophecy and he called false prophecy, and that he had long hesitated to write against it for fear of seeming to add to the Gospel of the New Testament. We know the movement mostly from its opponents."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 5.16"
  - date: "c. AD 177 (dating debated)"
    title: "The persecution at Lyons and Vienne"
    summary: "The churches of Vienne and Lyons in Gaul wrote a letter to the churches of Asia and Phrygia about the sufferings of their members, and Eusebius quotes it at length. It tells of Pothinus, bishop of Lyons, who was more than ninety years old and died after he was dragged before the judge. The translator of our edition argues that the letter does not justify describing the persecution as wholesale."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 5.1 (letter of the churches of Vienne and Lyons, with the translator's notes)"
  - date: "c. AD 175 to 180 (debated)"
    title: "Celsus writes The True Word against Christianity"
    summary: "The Greek philosopher Celsus argued that Christians formed secret associations against the law and spread their teaching among the uneducated. His book is lost, and it survives only in the long reply that Origen wrote in the next century."
    source: "Origen, Against Celsus 1.1, 3.55"
  - date: "c. AD 180"
    title: "Irenaeus writes Against Heresies"
    summary: "Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, answers Valentinus, Marcion and other teachers by appealing to the churches that the apostles founded and to the tradition handed down in them. He also argues that the Gospels cannot be more or fewer than four, because there are four zones of the world and four principal winds."
    source: "Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.1 to 3.4, 3.11.8"
  - date: "c. AD 170 to 200 (debated)"
    title: "The Muratorian fragment lists books of the New Testament"
    summary: "A damaged Latin list, found in a manuscript in Milan and named after the man who published it in 1740, which names Luke as the third Gospel and John as the fourth, and lists Acts, thirteen letters of Paul, and the Apocalypse. It says Hermas wrote in Rome in the time of Pius and that the book ought to be read but not placed among the prophets or the apostles, and it rejects letters written in Paul's name against Marcion. The translator of the edition we use thinks the writer belongs to the latter half of the second century; other scholars argue for the fourth century, so the date is debated."
    source: "Muratorian fragment (ANF vol. 5, with the translator's notes)"
  - date: "c. AD 190 to 195"
    title: "Easter: Victor of Rome and the churches of Asia"
    summary: "The churches of Asia kept the Lord's passover on the fourteenth day of the Jewish month, on whatever day of the week it fell; the other churches kept it only on Sunday. Eusebius reports that Victor, bishop of Rome, tried to cut off all of Asia, and that other bishops, among them Irenaeus, rebuked him. Irenaeus recalls that Polycarp and Anicetus of Rome, who disagreed on this, still made peace with each other."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 5.23, 5.24"
  - date: "c. AD 197 to 200 (debated)"
    title: "Tertullian defends Christians in his Apology"
    summary: "Tertullian, a Latin writer in Carthage, wrote an Apology addressed to Roman authorities in which he says the more Christians are mown down, the more they grow. The blood of Christians, he says, is seed."
    source: "Tertullian, Apology 50"
  - date: "c. AD 202"
    title: "Perpetua and Felicitas are martyred at Carthage"
    summary: "The Passion of Perpetua and Felicitas, which includes parts written by Perpetua herself and her companion Saturus, is a rare account written in part by a martyr herself. The translator's notes date it to the reign of Septimius Severus, about AD 202, and to Carthage, and say some think the narrator was a Montanist."
    source: "Passion of Perpetua and Felicitas (ANF vol. 3, with the translator's notes)"
  - date: "AD 250"
    title: "Decius's persecution: bishops arrested and many give way"
    summary: "Eusebius says that Decius, who hated his predecessor Philip, began a persecution of the churches in which Fabian was martyred at Rome and Alexander of Jerusalem and Babylas of Antioch died in prison after confessing. Cyprian's letters and his treatise On the Lapsed deal with what followed: many Christians had sacrificed to the gods, or had got false certificates, and the church had to decide how to take them back. Many historians call this the first persecution to reach the whole empire; Eusebius does not describe it that way."
    source: "Eusebius, Church History 6.39; Cyprian, Epistle 10 and On the Lapsed"
  - date: "c. AD 251"
    title: "The lapsed, Novatus and the unity of the church"
    summary: "Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, wrote On the Unity of the Church to hold his church together, insisting that anyone cut off from the church is a stranger and an enemy and cannot have God as Father who does not have the church as mother. Eusebius records the dispute under the name of Novatus (Novatian). The rule for taking back those who had given way, and whether to rebaptise those baptised by heretics, divided church leaders; Eusebius says Cyprian and the bishops with him were the first to teach that it was necessary."
    source: "Cyprian, On the Unity of the Church 6; Eusebius, Church History 6.43, 7.3"
  - date: "c. AD 230 to 256 (debated)"
    title: "A house church at Dura-Europos"
    summary: "At Dura-Europos, a Roman garrison town on the Euphrates in Syria, archaeologists found a private house that had been rebuilt for Christian use, with a room that served as a baptistery decorated with paintings. It is the earliest church building that has been identified. The town fell to Persian attack in the AD 250s."
    source: "Excavation report: Kraeling, The Christian Building (see Further reading). Not yet checked."
  - date: "AD 257 to 258"
    title: "Valerian's order against bishops"
    summary: "Cyprian writes that Valerian had sent a rescript to the Senate: bishops, elders and deacons were to be punished at once; senators and knights were to lose their rank and property and, if they persisted, their heads; and women were to lose their property and be exiled. He reports that Xistus, the bishop of Rome, was killed in the cemetery with four deacons."
    source: "Cyprian, Epistle 81 (ANF numbering)"
  - date: "AD 303 to 311"
    title: "The Great Persecution begins at Nicomedia"
    summary: "Lactantius says that on the festival of Terminus, 23 February, in the eighth consulship of Diocletian and the seventh of Maximian, the prefect and officers broke into the church in Nicomedia, burned the books of Scripture and looted its furnishings, and soldiers then razed the building within a few hours while Diocletian and Galerius watched from the palace. "
    source: "Lactantius, Of the Manner in Which the Persecutors Died 12"
  - date: "AD 311"
    title: "Galerius gives Christians leave to exist again"
    summary: "In an edict preserved by Lactantius, Galerius says the emperors had tried to bring Christians back to the ancient institutions, that many had given way, but that many still refused both to worship the gods and to worship their own God, and that he therefore permits them to be Christians again and to hold their meetings, provided that they do not break the peace. They are to pray to their God for the emperor and for the state."
    source: "Lactantius 34"
  - date: "AD 312"
    title: "Constantine and the Milvian bridge"
    summary: "Lactantius says that when Constantine camped by the Milvian bridge before the battle with Maxentius, he was told in a dream to mark the heavenly sign on his soldiers' shields. Eusebius gives a different account, in which the emperor told him long afterwards that he had seen a cross of light in the sky at midday with the words Conquer by this."
    source: "Lactantius 44; Eusebius, Life of Constantine 1.28"
  - date: "AD 313"
    title: "The Milan agreement of Constantine and Licinius"
    summary: "Lactantius preserves the text of a document issued at Nicomedia on the ides of June, which says Constantine and Licinius had met at Milan and agreed that Christians and everyone else should be free to follow the religion each chose. It orders places of worship and church property to be returned to the Christians. It is often called the Edict of Milan, but Lactantius's text was issued at Nicomedia, is addressed to a provincial governor, and protects all religions."
    source: "Lactantius 48"

## Overview

This era begins where the Apostolic Age ends. The apostles are gone, and the churches are left with their letters, with memories of the apostles, and with leaders called bishops, elders and deacons. It ends with the first Christian emperor. Between those two points lie about two hundred years in which Christianity grew from a small and mostly unnoticed movement into one that Roman emperors tried to destroy and then decided to tolerate.

The evidence is much richer than for the first century, and the story is much less tidy. Writers now argue with each other, with outsiders, and with Christians they regard as dangerous. Most of what we know of the groups they attacked, such as Marcion, the Montanists and the Gnostic teachers, comes from those who attacked them. Dates are often uncertain, and the timeline below says so wherever they are.

The page is built in two parts. The first runs to about AD 200, a period of letters, first outside views, and arguments over what counts as Scripture. The second runs from AD 200 to 313, a period of larger and better-organised churches, empire-wide persecutions, and finally toleration.

## Key beliefs and debates

### What did "catholic" mean?

The word comes from Greek and means "universal". Ignatius uses it in his letter to Smyrna to say that wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the catholic church (Ignatius, Smyrnaeans 8). He is not naming a denomination. He is making an argument about unity: the church is whole wherever the bishop, the elders and the deacons are together. The Muratorian fragment speaks of books that cannot be received into the Catholic Church. When a modern reader sees the word in this period, "universal" is the safe meaning.

### Bishops and unity

The first century used several titles for leaders, and scholars debate when a single bishop came to head each church. Ignatius insists on it more strongly than anyone before him: follow the bishop as Jesus Christ follows the Father, he tells the Smyrnaeans, and let nothing be done without the bishop (Smyrnaeans 8). Irenaeus builds his argument against Marcion and the Gnostic teachers on the churches the apostles founded and the bishops who followed them (Against Heresies 3.3). Cyprian, a century later, goes furthest: one who does not have the church for his mother cannot have God for his father (On the Unity of the Church 6). Scholars disagree about how general the single bishop was before Ignatius, and how far his own churches matched what he asks for.

### How did the New Testament take shape?

The Apostolic Age ended with a collection of writings in circulation but no agreed list. By the end of this era there is something close to one. The path was not straight, and historians differ on how important any single step was.

Marcion made a short list of his own: an edited Luke and edited letters of Paul, with the prophets and the law set aside (Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.27.2). Irenaeus answers by defending four Gospels, which he says cannot be more or fewer, and he gives a reason from the four zones of the world and the four winds (Against Heresies 3.11.8). The Muratorian fragment, which may be from the late second century or the fourth, names Luke as the third Gospel and John as the fourth, lists Acts, thirteen letters of Paul and the Apocalypse, and says the Shepherd of Hermas should be read but not placed among the prophets or the apostles. 2 Peter itself, which may be one of the latest writings, speaks of Paul's letters alongside "the other scriptures" (2 Peter 3:15 to 16).

Harry Gamble describes a gradual process in which churches settled on the books they already used in worship, with outside pressure from Marcion and others sharpening the question. How much weight to give Marcion is debated. See Further reading.

### Gnostic teachers

Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* is our main source on Valentinus and others whom he calls heretics, and his aim is to refute them. For a long time historians had little else. That changed in 1945, when a collection of writings in Coptic was found at Nag Hammadi in Egypt, including works by authors Irenaeus would have called heretics. Scholars use them to ask how fairly Irenaeus described his opponents and how different the groups really were. Historians disagree about how much to rely on these texts and about whether "Gnosticism" is a useful single label.

### Montanism

Montanus began to prophesy in Phrygia, and his followers were criticised by those who thought prophecy had no place after the apostles. The critic quoted by Eusebius writes that he hesitated to refute them for fear of seeming to add to the Gospel of the New Testament, and that the movement "separated" them from the church (Eusebius, Church History 5.16). We know the movement through its opponents. The editor of the *Passion of Perpetua* says some have thought the martyrs and the writer were Montanists, so the question reaches into the sources we have.

### Why was Easter disputed?

The churches of Asia kept the passover on the fourteenth day of the Jewish month, whatever day of the week it fell, while most others kept it on a Sunday (Eusebius, Church History 5.23). The two sides each claimed apostolic tradition. Polycarp visited Rome on the question in the time of Anicetus, and they agreed to differ (Church History 5.24). Victor, a generation later, tried to excommunicate all of Asia and was rebuked by other bishops, including Irenaeus. It shows both how strong the pull to uniformity was and how many churches would resist a bishop of Rome.

### What did outsiders think?

Pliny's letter is the earliest official Roman view that survives. He does not call Christians criminals for any particular act. He asks whether the name itself is punishable, and calls their worship a "contagious superstition" (Letters 10.96). Trajan tells him not to hunt Christians out and not to accept anonymous accusations. Celsus mocks Christians as people who work in wool and leather and speak to children in private houses (Origen, Against Celsus 3.55), and he accuses them of meeting in secret against the law (1.1). Robert Wilken's *The Christians as the Romans Saw Them* gathers the views of Pliny, Celsus and other pagan writers, and argues that educated Romans took Christianity seriously enough to attack it. See Further reading.

### How much were Christians persecuted?

Tradition counts ten persecutions, from Nero to Diocletian. Augustine reports this list as the opinion of "some", names the emperors, and says he does not think the number was foretold by the ten plagues of Egypt (City of God 18.52). Many modern historians think that before Decius persecution was local and occasional: Pliny shows an official working out what to do case by case, and the Lyons letter concerns one province. The translator of our edition of Eusebius warns that the Lyons persecution should not be called wholesale. Decius in AD 250, Valerian in AD 257 to 258 and Diocletian from AD 303 are different: Eusebius, Cyprian and Lactantius describe measures aimed at bishops, clergy and church property in many places.

Candida Moss argues in *The Myth of Christian Persecution* that later Christians overstated how often and how widely Christians were killed. Others reply that the sources for Decius, Valerian and Diocletian are strong. The disagreement is partly about numbers, which no source gives, and partly about how to read martyr stories. See Further reading.

### The Edict of Milan

The agreement of AD 313 is the best-known date of the period, and it is often misdescribed. The text we have is an edict that Lactantius says Licinius had published at Nicomedia, addressed to a provincial governor, in which Constantine and Licinius say they had met at Milan and agreed on religious freedom for Christians and everyone else (Lactantius 48). The main toleration decree is two years earlier, Galerius's edict of AD 311 (Lactantius 34). Neither made Christianity the religion of the empire.

## Worship and community life

We know more about worship now than in the first century, mostly from one source, Justin's description for the emperor, written to show outsiders that Christians had nothing to hide.

### Baptism

Justin says those who are persuaded and promise to live by what Christians teach are taught to pray and fast for forgiveness, and are then brought to water and washed in the name of God the Father, of Jesus Christ, and of the Holy Spirit (First Apology 61). He adds that the baptised are brought to the brethren to pray together (First Apology 65). The Didache, from an earlier time, allows pouring if there is not enough running water.

### Sunday

On the day called Sunday, Justin says, everyone in town or country gathers in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read as long as time allows. The president speaks to urge them to imitate these good things; everyone stands to pray; bread, wine and water are brought; the president prays and gives thanks as well as he can; the people say Amen; and each receives a share, and a portion is sent by the deacons to those who are absent (First Apology 67). The well-off give what they choose, and the collection, kept by the president, helps orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers.

### The Lord's Supper

Justin explains that they do not take the bread and wine as ordinary food and drink. He compares it to the incarnation: as Jesus was made flesh, so the food blessed by prayer is the flesh and blood of that Jesus (First Apology 66). He says the apostles in their memoirs, which are called Gospels, handed down what Jesus enjoined. Christians continue to read these differently, and this page does not take a side.

### Where did Christians meet?

Pliny says Christians met on a fixed day before dawn, addressed a prayer to Christ as to a god and shared a harmless meal (Letters 10.96). In the first century believers met in private houses. At Dura-Europos in Syria, archaeologists found a house rebuilt for Christian use with a room used for baptism, which is the earliest church building identified. It is dated to the AD 230s to 250s, before the town fell to a Persian attack. Lactantius shows that by AD 303 at least one city, Nicomedia, had a church building prominent enough to be seen from the emperor's palace (Lactantius 12).

### Leaders and discipline

Ignatius pictures a bishop, elders and deacons together, with the Eucharist and baptism under the bishop's authority (Smyrnaeans 8). By Cyprian's time the question is what to do with those who gave way in persecution. He writes that some had defiled their conscience with "wicked certificates" and urges the martyrs and confessors in prison to keep to the discipline of the Gospel (Epistle 10). Cyprian's treatise On the Lapsed ends with a warning against the Novatians, the followers of Novatus.

## Glossary

| --- | --- |
| Apology | A defence of Christianity addressed to outsiders, such as Justin's to the emperor and Tertullian's to the Roman authorities |
| Bishop | From the Greek *episkopos*, overseer. In this era, the head of a local church |
