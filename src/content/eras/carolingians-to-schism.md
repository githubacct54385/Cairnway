## Overview

The fifth era ended at the Second Council of Nicaea in 787. This era begins with the Frankish answer to that council and ends with the excommunication of the patriarch of Constantinople by papal legates in 1054. In between, a Frankish king was crowned emperor in Rome, his empire was divided among his grandsons, the eastern church restored its icons, Rome and Constantinople quarrelled over Photius and the creed, and a monastery was founded at Cluny under the protection of the pope and subject to no earthly power.

The years from 787 to 800 open the era. The Frankish church had taken no part in the council of 787 and read its acts in a faulty Latin translation. The Caroline Books, written in Charlemagne's name about 790 to 792, rejected both the council of 787 and the iconoclast council of 754, and the council of Frankfurt repudiated the council of 787 in 794. Charlemagne's coronation in Rome in 800 followed.

## Key debates

### What did the coronation of 800 mean?

The Book of the Popes describes the pope crowning Charles and the Romans hailing him. Einhard says Charles would not have entered the church that day had he known the pope's plan. Grant, the editor of Einhard, argues in a note that the regret was more probably about the manner, a title that seemed to come as a gift from the papacy, than about the title itself. The source book of Thatcher and McNeal says Charles had wanted the title and sought recognition from Constantinople, which came in 812. The sources read here do not settle whether the coronation was the pope's idea or Charles's.

### Who should choose the pope?

In the sources read here, the choice was often made by others than the clergy of Rome. Thatcher and McNeal say the papacy had fallen under the control of Roman factions before 1046. After a synod held by Otto I in 963, the Romans elected Leo VIII in place of John XII, and at Sutri in 1046 Henry III had three rival claimants removed and a German bishop chosen. The Annales Romani add that the Romans then granted Henry the right to choose popes. Leo IX, chosen with Henry's backing in 1049, began a reform of the clergy.

### Should the Filioque be in the creed?

Western churches added to the creed that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father "and the Son", in Latin *Filioque*. The Catholic Encyclopedia says no one knows who first added it, but that the creed with the addition was first sung in Spain after the Goths' conversion, professed at Toledo in 589. A synod at Friuli adopted it in 796, and the council of Aachen of 809 appears to have approved it; Pope Leo III approved the doctrine but advised leaving the phrase out of the creed. The practice continued, and the encyclopedia says it was established in Rome itself by the middle of the eleventh century. Photius opposed the addition and listed it among his charges against the Latins in 867. The page reports the dispute but does not give the Eastern and Western arguments in full.

## Worship and community life

### Monks and reform

Cluny's charter set its monks under the Rule of St Benedict, let them elect their own abbot, and placed them under the protection of the pope. It also bound them to works of mercy for the poor, strangers and pilgrims. Ogg's introduction says Cluny kept the houses its monks founded under its own abbot, building a congregation of many monasteries.

### Schools, books and worship

Charlemagne's letter of 787 to the abbot of Fulda urged monks to study letters so they could understand the Scriptures. Einhard says Charles reformed the manner of reading and singing in church. The Catholic Encyclopedia credits Alcuin with a revised text of the Latin Vulgate and with the Mass-book that the Frankish church adopted. In the East, Cyril and Methodius gave the Slavs an alphabet and a liturgy in their own language.
