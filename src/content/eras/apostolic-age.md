keyPeople:
  - name: "Peter (Cephas)"
    role: "Early leader among Jesus's followers"
    summary: "Named first in Paul's list of those to whom the risen Jesus appeared. In Acts he speaks for the group in Jerusalem and, at the Jerusalem meeting, says non-Jewish believers are saved by grace in the same way as Jewish believers. Paul also records a dispute with him in Antioch, where Paul says Peter stopped eating with non-Jewish believers once people came from James. Acts says nothing of his later life; later tradition places his death in Rome."
    source: "1 Corinthians 15:5; Galatians 1:18, 2:7 to 14; Acts 1 to 12, 15:6 to 11; for his death, see Key events"
  - name: "Paul"
    role: "Missionary and letter writer"
    summary: "A Jewish teacher who writes that he once persecuted the church and then began preaching Jesus to non-Jews. His letters are among the earliest surviving Christian writings, and in one he lists years of hardship: beatings, a stoning, shipwrecks and imprisonment. Acts tells his story in a longer narrative that differs from his own account in some details: Paul says that after his change he went to Arabia and, on his first visit to Jerusalem, saw only Peter and James, while Acts has Barnabas bring him to the apostles. Acts ends with Paul under guard in Rome; later tradition says he died there."
    source: "Galatians 1:13 to 19; 2 Corinthians 11:23 to 28; Acts 9 to 28 (compare 9:26 to 27)"
  - name: "James"
    role: "Leader of the Jerusalem community"
    summary: "Paul calls him the Lord's brother and says he met him in Jerusalem. In Acts, James gives the judgment that ends the Jerusalem meeting: non-Jewish believers should not be burdened, but should avoid a short list of practices. Josephus reports that he was stoned at the order of the high priest Ananus. Christian traditions differ on what brother means here, such as a full sibling, half-brother or cousin."
    source: "Galatians 1:19, 2:9 and 12; 1 Corinthians 15:7; Acts 15:13 to 21; Josephus, Antiquities 20.9.1"
  - name: "Barnabas"
    role: "Early teacher and Paul's travelling partner"
    summary: "Acts introduces him as Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus nicknamed Barnabas, which it translates as Son of Encouragement. The church at Antioch sent him out with Paul, and Paul mentions him in Galatians as someone who was drawn into the Antioch dispute."
    source: "Acts 4:36 to 37, 13:1 to 3, 15:12; Galatians 2:13"
  - name: "Priscilla (Prisca) and Aquila"
    role: "Co-workers of Paul"
    summary: "A married couple who, according to Acts, left Rome when Claudius ordered Jews out, worked as tentmakers in Corinth alongside Paul, and later explained the faith more fully to the teacher Apollos in Ephesus. Paul greets them in his letters, says they risked their necks for him, and sends greetings from the assembly that met in their house."
    source: "Acts 18:1 to 3, 18 to 26; Romans 16:3 to 5; 1 Corinthians 16:19"
  - name: "John, son of Zebedee"
    role: "One of the twelve; a pillar of the Jerusalem church"
    summary: "A Galilean fisherman and the brother of James, the son of Zebedee. In Acts he appears beside Peter in Jerusalem and Samaria, and Paul names him with James and Peter as one of the pillars who agreed that Paul should go to non-Jews. That is nearly all the first-century evidence says about him. Later tradition credits him with the Gospel of John, the three letters of John and Revelation, and says he lived to old age in Ephesus. Most modern scholars doubt that he wrote these books, and they debate the identity of the beloved disciple in John's Gospel, whom tradition took to be him."
    source: "Mark 1:19 to 20; Acts 3:1 to 4:22, 8:14 to 25; Galatians 2:9; John 21:20 to 24; Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.1.1, 3.3.4 (later tradition)"
  - name: "Stephen"
    role: "One of the seven; the first martyr in Acts"
    summary: "Acts presents him as one of seven men chosen to oversee the food distribution in the Jerusalem community, all with Greek names, and describes him as full of faith and the Holy Spirit. His long speech in Acts retells the history of Israel and criticises the temple. As he is stoned, Acts has him pray for his killers, echoing the words of Jesus at the crucifixion in Luke. Acts is the only source for him."
    source: "Acts 6:1 to 8:2; Luke 23:34 and 46"
  - name: "Timothy"
    role: "Paul's co-worker and envoy"
    summary: "Acts says he came from Lystra, the son of a Jewish believing mother and a Greek father, and joined Paul on his second journey. Paul names him as co-sender of several letters and sends him to churches on his behalf, calling him like a son. Two letters in the New Testament are addressed to him, though many scholars think they were written after Paul's death in his name."
    source: "Acts 16:1 to 3; 1 Thessalonians 1:1, 3:2 to 6; 1 Corinthians 4:17; Philippians 1:1, 2:19 to 22; Philemon 1; 1 and 2 Timothy"
  - name: "Apollos"
    role: "Teacher from Alexandria"
    summary: "Acts describes him as a Jew from Alexandria, eloquent and well versed in the scriptures, who taught about Jesus in Ephesus while knowing only the baptism of John, until Priscilla and Aquila explained the way of God to him more fully. He then worked in Corinth. Paul mentions him several times in 1 Corinthians, where some believers rallied around him as a rival to Paul. Paul plays this down: he planted, he writes, and Apollos watered. Some later readers, starting with Martin Luther, guessed that he wrote Hebrews, but this is speculation."
    source: "Acts 18:24 to 19:1; 1 Corinthians 1:12, 3:4 to 9, 16:12"
  - name: "Phoebe"
    role: "Leader in the church at Cenchreae"
    summary: "Paul commends her to the church in Rome as our sister and a diakonos of the church at Cenchreae, the port of Corinth, and says she has been a prostatis, a patron or helper, of many people and of Paul himself. Translations render diakonos as servant, minister or deacon, and scholars debate whether it was already a title of office. Because Paul asks the Romans to welcome her, many scholars think she carried the letter."
    source: "Romans 16:1 to 2"
  - name: "Andronicus and Junia"
    role: "Early believers Paul calls outstanding among the apostles"
    summary: "Paul greets them as his relatives and fellow prisoners, who were believers before he was, and who are outstanding among the apostles. Two questions are debated. Early Christian writers such as John Chrysostom read Junia as a woman's name, and most modern translations do too, while some older ones print a male name, Junias, which is not otherwise known. And the Greek phrase can mean either that they were notable apostles themselves or that they were well known to the apostles. Scholars disagree on both."
    source: "Romans 16:7; John Chrysostom, Homilies on Romans 31 (later tradition)"
events:
  - date: "c. AD 30 to 33 (dating debated)"
    title: "Jesus is executed under Pontius Pilate"
    summary: "Christians date their movement to the crucifixion of Jesus. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing about 80 years later, reports that the founder of the Christians, Christus, was executed under Tiberius by the procurator Pontius Pilate. An inscription found at Caesarea gives Pilate's actual title as prefect. Scholars debate how much of this came from Christian tradition and how much from Roman records. Scholars place the year at about AD 30 or 33."
    source: "Tacitus, Annals 15.44; the four Gospels"
  - date: "c. AD 30 to 33 (dating debated)"
    title: "The first Jerusalem community"
    summary: "According to Acts, the followers of Jesus gathered in Jerusalem and the community grew after the festival of Pentecost. Acts is our main narrative for these years, and it was written decades afterward."
    source: "Acts 1 to 2"
  - date: "c. AD 31 to 36 (dating debated)"
    title: "Stephen is killed, and the believers scatter"
    summary: "Acts says the Jerusalem community chose seven men, Stephen among them, to oversee the daily distribution of food after complaints that the Greek-speaking widows were being overlooked. It then tells how Stephen was accused of speaking against the temple and the law, gave a long speech before the council, and was stoned outside the city. Acts says a young man named Saul, the later Paul, watched and approved. In Acts this is the turning point that sends believers out of Jerusalem into Judea and Samaria, with Philip preaching in Samaria and to an Ethiopian official. Stephen is the first person Acts presents as dying for his faith. Acts is the only source for these events."
    source: "Acts 6 to 8 (especially 6:1 to 6, 7:58 to 8:4, 8:26 to 40)"
  - date: "c. AD 33 to 36 (dating debated)"
    title: "Paul begins following Jesus"
    summary: "Paul, who says he had opposed the movement, writes that God revealed Jesus to him and sent him to preach to non-Jews. Acts tells the story of his turning on the road to Damascus three times, and even these tellings differ: in one, the men with him hear the voice, and in another they do not."
    source: "Galatians 1:11 to 17; Acts 9:1 to 19, 22:6 to 16, 26:12 to 18 (compare 9:7 and 22:9)"
  - date: "c. AD 36 to 39 (dating debated)"
    title: "Paul visits Peter and James in Jerusalem"
    summary: "Paul writes that three years after his change he went to Jerusalem, stayed fifteen days with Peter, and saw James, the brother of the Lord, but no other apostle. In another letter Paul tells how he escaped from Damascus, let down in a basket through a window in the city wall, while the governor under King Aretas was guarding the city to seize him. Paul does not say exactly when this happened, but Acts tells the basket escape just before this visit, though it blames a plot by Jews in Damascus. Aretas IV, king of the Nabataeans, died around AD 40, so the escape came before then, which helps date Paul's early career."
    source: "Galatians 1:18 to 20; 2 Corinthians 11:32 to 33; Acts 9:23 to 26"
  - date: "c. AD 40 to 45 (dating debated)"
    title: "Believers are first called Christians at Antioch"
    summary: "Acts says that believers scattered after Stephen's death reached Antioch in Syria, one of the largest cities of the eastern empire, and that some of them began speaking to Greeks as well as Jews. The Jerusalem church sent Barnabas, who fetched Paul from Tarsus, and the two taught there for a year. Acts says that it was in Antioch that the disciples were first called Christians, a name probably given by outsiders. Antioch became the base for Paul's journeys and the scene of his dispute with Peter. Acts also says the Antioch church sent famine relief to Jerusalem in the reign of Claudius, and Josephus mentions a famine in Judea in the mid-40s."
    source: "Acts 11:19 to 30, 13:1 to 3; Galatians 2:11 to 14; Josephus, Antiquities 20.2.5 (20.51 to 53) and 20.5.2 (20.101)"
  - date: "AD 41 to 44, probably c. AD 44"
    title: "Herod Agrippa I executes James, the son of Zebedee"
    summary: "Acts says King Herod, meaning Herod Agrippa I, the grandson of Herod the Great, had James the brother of John killed with the sword and then arrested Peter, who Acts says was freed from prison by an angel. Apart from Judas, James is the only one of the twelve apostles whose death Acts records. Acts then describes Herod's sudden death at Caesarea after a crowd hailed him as a god. Josephus tells a similar story of Agrippa's death at Caesarea, also with the crowd calling him a god, and places it after he had ruled all of Judea for three years. Agrippa ruled Judea from AD 41, so historians usually date his death to AD 44, which makes this one of the few events in Acts with an outside date. Acts does not say exactly how long before Agrippa's death James was killed."
    source: "Acts 12:1 to 23; Josephus, Antiquities 19.8.2 (19.343 to 352)"
  - date: "c. AD 49 (dating debated)"
    title: "A meeting in Jerusalem about non-Jewish believers"
    summary: "Acts 15 describes a meeting about whether non-Jewish believers needed to follow Jewish law. Paul describes a related meeting in Galatians 2, and scholars debate how the two accounts relate."
    source: "Acts 15; Galatians 2"
  - date: "AD 49, or within AD 41 to 53 (dating debated)"
    title: "Claudius expels Jews from Rome"
    summary: "Acts says that Aquila and Priscilla had left Italy because Claudius ordered Jews to leave Rome. Suetonius reports that Claudius expelled Jews who were causing disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus. Scholars disagree about the year, how many people were affected, and whether Chrestus refers to Christ."
    source: "Acts 18:2; Suetonius, Life of Claudius 25.4"
  - date: "c. AD 50 to 52 (dating debated)"
    title: "Paul in Corinth before the proconsul Gallio"
    summary: "Acts places Paul in Corinth during the term of the Roman governor Gallio, the brother of the philosopher Seneca. An inscription found at Delphi, a letter of the emperor Claudius that names Gallio as proconsul, lets historians date his term and so anchor Paul's career. Most land at about AD 51 to 52, and some argue for slightly different years."
    source: "Acts 18:1 to 17 (Gallio at 18:12 to 17); the Gallio inscription at Delphi"
  - date: "c. AD 56 to 58 (dating debated)"
    title: "Paul writes to a church in Rome he has never visited"
    summary: "Paul's letter to the Romans is addressed to believers in a city he says he has long wanted to visit but has not yet reached. So there were already Christians in Rome, and nobody knows who first brought the message there. Acts lists visitors from Rome among the crowd at Pentecost, and the expulsion under Claudius may show that disputes about Christ had reached the Jewish community in Rome in the 40s. Paul greets many people by name at the end of the letter, including Prisca and Aquila, and says he plans to stop in Rome on his way to Spain. Later tradition names Peter as the founder of the Roman church, but the letter does not mention him."
    source: "Romans 1:7 to 15, 15:22 to 29, 16:1 to 16; Acts 2:10; Suetonius, Life of Claudius 25.4"
  - date: "c. AD 57 to 60 (dating debated)"
    title: "Paul is arrested in Jerusalem and appeals to Caesar"
    summary: "Acts says that when Paul brought his collection to Jerusalem, he was accused of taking a non-Jew into the inner temple courts. A riot followed and Roman soldiers took him into custody. He was moved to Caesarea and held there for two years under the governor Felix. When Festus replaced Felix, Paul, as a Roman citizen, appealed to have his case heard by the emperor, and after a hearing before King Agrippa II he was sent to Rome. Felix and Festus are both known from Josephus. Most scholars place the change of governor in about AD 59 or 60, which helps to date the end of Paul's career, though some argue for a few years earlier. Paul's own letters do not describe these events."
    source: "Acts 21:17 to 26:32 (especially 21:27 to 36, 24:27, 25:10 to 12); Romans 15:25 to 31; Josephus, Antiquities 20.8 (20.137 to 182)"
  - date: "c. AD 62"
    title: "James, the brother of Jesus, is killed in Jerusalem"
    summary: "Josephus reports that the high priest Ananus brought James, whom he calls the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, before a council and had him stoned. Most scholars regard this passage as authentic, in contrast with a more disputed passage about Jesus elsewhere in Josephus. Josephus adds that some people in Jerusalem objected, and that King Agrippa II removed Ananus as high priest after only three months. A different account, from the second-century Christian writer Hegesippus and preserved by Eusebius, says James was thrown down from the temple, stoned and beaten to death, and places his death just before the Roman siege of Jerusalem. Josephus wrote nearer the time."
    source: "Josephus, Antiquities 20.9.1 (20.197 to 203); Eusebius, Church History 2.23 (quoting Hegesippus; later tradition)"
  - date: "AD 64"
    title: "Nero blames Christians after the fire of Rome"
    summary: "Tacitus reports that Nero blamed a group called Christians for the great fire at Rome and had many of them punished. Tacitus wrote decades later and suggests Nero was deflecting blame from himself, while describing Christians as widely disliked."
    source: "Tacitus, Annals 15.44"
  - date: "c. AD 64 to 68 (later tradition; dating debated)"
    title: "Peter and Paul die in Rome"
    summary: "Acts ends with Paul spending two years in Rome under guard and does not say what happened to him, and the New Testament does not describe the death of either apostle. The earliest text that seems to refer to their deaths is 1 Clement, a letter from the church in Rome written about AD 96, which holds up Peter and Paul as examples who suffered and bore witness. It does not say where, how or when they died. The Gospel of John has Jesus hint at the kind of death Peter would die, and around AD 110 Ignatius of Antioch, writing to the Roman church, mentions Peter and Paul as having given it instructions. Later tradition says more. From the late second century on, Christian writers say both apostles taught in Rome and died there, and some say it was under Nero. Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, reports that Paul was beheaded and Peter crucified, and, citing the earlier writer Origen, that Peter asked to be crucified head downward. Most scholars accept that both apostles died in Rome, probably under Nero, while a minority doubt that Peter was ever there. A separate tradition, based on Paul's stated plan to visit Spain and on 1 Clement's phrase that he reached the limit of the west, holds that he was released and travelled again before his death. Scholars debate this too."
    source: "Acts 28:30 to 31; John 21:18 to 19; Romans 15:24; 1 Clement 5; Ignatius, Letter to the Romans 4.3; Eusebius, Church History 2.25 and 3.1 (later tradition)"
  - date: "c. AD 66 to 68 (later tradition; debated)"
    title: "The Jewish revolt, and the Jerusalem church leaves for Pella"
    summary: "In AD 66 a revolt against Rome broke out in Judea, the war Josephus later described as an eyewitness. Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, says that before the war the Jerusalem believers were warned by a revelation to leave the city and settled in Pella, a town east of the Jordan. A later writer, Epiphanius, tells the same story. Some scholars accept that a group of Jerusalem believers moved to Pella, while others doubt it, noting that the account is late and that Pella itself was attacked early in the war. The first-century sources do not mention it."
    source: "Josephus, Jewish War 2 (the outbreak of the revolt); Eusebius, Church History 3.5.3 (later tradition)"
  - date: "AD 70"
    title: "The Jerusalem temple is destroyed"
    summary: "During the Jewish revolt, Roman forces under Titus captured Jerusalem and the temple burned. Josephus dates it to the month of Av and says Titus tried to save it. Other ancient writers say he ordered it destroyed, and modern scholars disagree about which is right."
    source: "Josephus, Jewish War 6"
  - date: "AD 81 to 96 (debated)"
    title: "Did Domitian persecute Christians?"
    summary: "Christian writers from the late second century on, including Melito of Sardis and Tertullian, and later Eusebius, say that the emperor Domitian persecuted Christians, making him the second emperor to do so, after Nero. The Roman historian Cassius Dio, writing in the early third century, says Domitian executed his cousin Flavius Clemens and exiled Clemens's wife Domitilla on a charge of atheism and drifting into Jewish ways, and some scholars think they may have been Christians. Revelation and 1 Clement both hint at pressure on believers in these years. Many modern historians doubt there was any empire-wide campaign against Christians under Domitian and think pressure was local and occasional. The question is open."
    source: "Cassius Dio, Roman History 67.14; 1 Clement 1.1; Revelation 1:9, 2:13; Eusebius, Church History 3.17 to 20 and 4.26.9 (later tradition)"
  - date: "c. AD 95 (dating debated)"
    title: "Revelation is written, and the tradition of John at Ephesus"
    summary: "The book of Revelation says its author, John, received his visions on the island of Patmos, off the coast of Asia, because of the word of God and his testimony about Jesus. It opens with messages to seven churches in the province of Asia, including Ephesus, Smyrna and Pergamum. In the second century, Justin Martyr and Irenaeus identify this John with the apostle John, and Irenaeus places the visions late in Domitian's reign and says John lived on at Ephesus into the reign of Trajan. In the third century, Dionysius of Alexandria already doubted that the same person wrote Revelation and the Gospel of John, because their Greek is so different, and most modern scholars agree. Some date Revelation to the late 60s instead."
    source: "Revelation 1:1 to 11, 2 to 3; Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 81; Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.3.4 and 5.30.3; Eusebius, Church History 3.23, 7.25 (later tradition)"
  - date: "c. AD 96 (dating debated)"
    title: "The church in Rome writes to the church in Corinth"
    summary: "A letter now called 1 Clement was sent from the church in Rome to the church in Corinth after some of the Corinthian leaders, whom it calls presbyters or elders, had been removed from office. The letter urges the Corinthians to restore them. It argues that the apostles appointed the first leaders and gave instructions for others to succeed them, which makes it an early witness to how church leadership was becoming more settled. The letter does not name its author. Later tradition credits it to Clement, a leader of the Roman church. Most scholars date it to about AD 95 to 97, partly because it mentions sudden misfortunes that some link to the reign of the emperor Domitian, though others argue for a date a few decades earlier or later. It marks the end of the first generation: the apostles are now remembered as figures of the past."
    source: "1 Clement 1.1, 42, 44, 47; Eusebius, Church History 4.23.11 (later tradition on the author)"

## Overview

The Apostolic Age is the first generation of Christians, from the years after the death of Jesus to the end of the first century. The name comes from the apostles, the early leaders whom the New Testament describes as sent out to teach.

According to the book of Acts, the first Christians were Jewish followers of Jesus who gathered in Jerusalem and then spread their message to other cities. Paul's own letters give a second view of the movement as it grew beyond Jewish communities into the wider Roman world. Scholars generally date Paul's letters to the middle of the first century, which makes them among the earliest surviving Christian writings.

Our written evidence is limited. Historians draw on Acts, Paul's letters, and a few mentions in Roman and Jewish writers such as Tacitus, Suetonius and Josephus. Those writers were not Christians and did not write to tell the Christian story, which is part of why they are useful. Historians debate the dating of many events here and how the sources fit together, and the timeline below flags each date that is uncertain.

## When were the sources written?

Most of the New Testament was written during this era, but not all at once, and not in the order it is printed. The
table gives the ranges scholars commonly suggest. Every date here is debated. Where authorship is disputed, the date
depends on who wrote the book, so both are shown. The ranges follow standard introductions to the New Testament, such
as Raymond E. Brown's (see the sources for this era), and none of the New Testament books states its own date.

## Key beliefs and debates

### What the first believers proclaimed

In a letter written about twenty years after the death of Jesus, Paul reminds the church in Corinth of what he says he passed on to them as received: that Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Peter, to the twelve, to a larger group, to James, to all the apostles, and finally to Paul himself (1 Corinthians 15:3 to 8). Believers called Jesus the Christ, which means the Anointed One, and Lord.

Paul's letters also show how early believers spoke to Jesus and about him. Paul keeps an Aramaic prayer addressed to Jesus, *Maranatha*, "our Lord, come" (1 Corinthians 16:22). He writes that for believers there is one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things exist. Those words echo the Jewish confession that God is one (1 Corinthians 8:6; compare Deuteronomy 6:4). And in a passage that may be an early hymn, he speaks of Christ being in the form of God, and of every knee bowing at the name of Jesus. Isaiah uses that last phrase of God himself (Philippians 2:6 to 11; compare Isaiah 45:23).

Scholars read this evidence differently. Larry Hurtado and Richard Bauckham argue that Jewish believers were devoted to Jesus alongside God within the first few years of the movement. James Dunn is more cautious: he argues that early believers honoured Jesus very highly but did not yet worship him in the same way as God. Bart Ehrman agrees that belief in Jesus's exalted status came early, but argues that it meant something different from the later creeds. Christians have traditionally read these passages as early witness to their belief that Jesus is divine. The books by each of these scholars are listed under further reading below.

### Did non-Jewish believers need to follow Jewish law?

This is the question the sources spend the most time on. Acts 15 describes a meeting in Jerusalem where Peter and James speak and the group concludes that non-Jewish believers should not be burdened, apart from a short list of practices. Paul's letter to the Galatians describes a meeting with Peter, James and John, and later a dispute with Peter in Antioch over eating with non-Jewish believers (Galatians 2). The two accounts differ in what they include, and scholars disagree about whether they describe the same meeting and how the decision was received. Galatians suggests the question stayed open after the meeting.

### How do Acts and Paul's letters fit together?

Acts and the letters sometimes differ in detail, for example in how many visits Paul made to Jerusalem and what happened at each. Many historians give priority to Paul's own letters, since he wrote them himself and they are earlier, and read Acts as a later narrative that has its own aims. Others give Acts more weight. In the timeline below, dates that depend on how this is settled are marked as debated.

### Which letters did Paul write?

Most scholars accept seven letters as Paul's own: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon. Scholars are divided on Colossians, Ephesians and 2 Thessalonians, with Ephesians most often thought to be by a later writer. Many consider 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus to have been written by later authors in Paul's name. Arguments here rest mostly on style, vocabulary and subject matter, and some scholars suggest that Paul's use of secretaries could explain differences in style. This page draws mainly on the seven letters most scholars accept.

### When did Christianity and Judaism become separate?

The first believers were Jewish, and Acts presents them meeting in the temple in Jerusalem. Paul says he was given the synagogue punishment of thirty-nine lashes five times (2 Corinthians 11:24), which suggests that he, and the synagogues that punished him, still treated him as a Jew under their discipline. John's Gospel, probably written near the end of the century, speaks of followers of Jesus being put out of the synagogue (John 9:22, 16:2). From the Roman side, Tacitus says that by AD 64 the people of Rome knew a group called Christians, whom Nero could single out. Soon after this era, Ignatius of Antioch became the first writer we know of to set "Christianity" against "Judaism" as two different things (Ignatius, Letter to the Magnesians 10).

Scholars draw different conclusions from this. James Dunn argued that the main "parting of the ways" came gradually, between the first and second Jewish revolts against Rome (AD 66 to 135). Others, in a collection titled *The Ways That Never Parted*, argue that the lines between Jews and Christians stayed blurred in many places for centuries. Much depends on the place, and on whether one asks how Christians saw themselves, how Jewish leaders saw them, or how Roman officials saw them.

## Worship and community life

Our sources say much less about what early Christians did when they met than about what they argued over, and most of what they say comes from Paul's letters, Acts, and a short church manual called the Didache. Practice probably differed from place to place.

### Meeting in houses

There were no church buildings. Believers met in private homes, and Paul greets the assemblies that met in the houses of Prisca and Aquila, of Nympha, and of Philemon (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15; Philemon 2). Acts describes the first Jerusalem believers attending the temple and breaking bread in their homes (Acts 2:46). A house could hold a few dozen people at most, so a city's believers may have met in several groups.

### Baptism

Acts presents baptism as the way new believers joined the community, from Pentecost onward (Acts 2:38 to 41). Paul describes it as being baptised into Christ's death and rising to a new life (Romans 6:3 to 4), and says that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male and female (Galatians 3:27 to 28). He also says he personally baptised only a few people in Corinth (1 Corinthians 1:14 to 17). The Didache prefers running water, allows pouring water three times on the head if there is not enough, and asks for fasting beforehand (Didache 7).

### The Lord's Supper

The earliest written account of the Lord's Supper is in 1 Corinthians, where Paul passes on what he says he received: on the night he was handed over, Jesus took bread and a cup and told his followers to do this in remembrance of him (1 Corinthians 11:23 to 26). In Corinth it was part of a shared meal, and Paul criticises the community because some ate and drank their fill while others went hungry (1 Corinthians 11:17 to 34). The Didache gives thanksgiving prayers over the cup and the bread (Didache 9 to 10). Scholars debate how and when the ritual came to be separated from an ordinary meal.

### The first day of the week

Paul asks the Corinthians to set money aside on the first day of every week (1 Corinthians 16:2), Acts has the believers in Troas gather on that day to break bread (Acts 20:7), and Revelation mentions the Lord's day (Revelation 1:10). The Didache tells believers to gather on the Lord's day (Didache 14). Jewish believers probably went on keeping the Sabbath as well.

### Prayer, singing and spiritual gifts

Paul describes meetings where each person might bring a hymn, a teaching, a revelation, a tongue or an interpretation (1 Corinthians 14:26), and he spends three chapters on prophecy, speaking in tongues and other gifts (1 Corinthians 12 to 14). He keeps one Aramaic prayer untranslated, Maranatha, meaning "our Lord, come" (1 Corinthians 16:22), which suggests it came from the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers. Many scholars think some passages in Paul's letters, such as Philippians 2:6 to 11, quote hymns that were already in use, though this is debated. The Didache tells believers to pray the Lord's Prayer three times a day (Didache 8).

### Sharing resources

Acts says the Jerusalem believers held their possessions in common and gave to anyone in need (Acts 2:44 to 45, 4:32 to 37). Paul spent years organising a collection from his non-Jewish churches for the poor among the believers in Jerusalem, and he saw it as a sign of unity between Jewish and non-Jewish believers (1 Corinthians 16:1 to 4; 2 Corinthians 8 to 9; Romans 15:25 to 28).

### Leaders

Titles for leaders were not yet fixed. Paul's letters mention apostles, prophets and teachers (1 Corinthians 12:28), and greet overseers and deacons in Philippi (Philippians 1:1). Acts has Paul and Barnabas appoint elders in each church (Acts 14:23). By the end of the century, 1 Clement and the Didache speak of overseers (in Greek *episkopoi*, the word that later became bishop), elders and deacons (1 Clement 42 to 44; Didache 15). Scholars debate when churches began to have a single bishop at their head. The letters of Ignatius, written soon after this era, are the first to insist on it.

### The Didache

The Didache, or "Teaching of the Twelve Apostles", is a short manual on Christian life and practice. It is not in the New Testament, and it was known only by name until a copy was found in a library in Constantinople in 1873 and published ten years later. Scholars date it anywhere from about AD 50 to 120, and many think it was put together in stages. It is our best evidence for how some early communities baptised, prayed, fasted and shared the Lord's Supper.

## Further reading

Modern scholarship is cited here, not copied. These books are a starting point, and they disagree with one another on purpose.

- *The Cambridge History of Christianity*, Volume 1: *Origins to Constantine*, edited by Margaret M. Mitchell and Frances M. Young (2006). A broad survey of the period.
- Raymond E. Brown, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (1997). Book-by-book dates, authorship and background.
- Larry W. Hurtado, *Lord Jesus Christ: Devotion to Jesus in Earliest Christianity* (2003). Argues for very early devotion to Jesus.
- Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the God of Israel* (2008). Argues that the earliest believers included Jesus in the identity of the one God.
- James D. G. Dunn, *Did the First Christians Worship Jesus? The New Testament Evidence* (2010). A more cautious reading of the same evidence.
- Bart D. Ehrman, *How Jesus Became God* (2014). Early belief in Jesus's exaltation, read as different from later creeds.
- James D. G. Dunn, *The Partings of the Ways* (second edition, 2006). How Christianity and Judaism came to be seen as separate.
- Adam H. Becker and Annette Yoshiko Reed, editors, *The Ways That Never Parted* (2003). Argues the lines stayed blurred for centuries.
