## Overview

The previous era ended with toleration. This one starts there. In AD 313 the emperors Constantine and Licinius agreed that Christians could worship freely, and within a few decades the emperor was a Christian and the bishops were meeting at his invitation. The questions the church argued over from then on were no longer how to survive but how to speak: who Christ is, which books to read in church, and what grace means.

The page is arranged by East and West, because the Greek-speaking and Latin-speaking halves of the empire argued about different things in different languages. In the East the main questions were about the Son and the Spirit and, later, about the person of Christ. In the West the main figures, Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine, wrote about church and state, Scripture, sin and grace.

This East and West is a matter of language and region. It is not the split between Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches, which is conventionally dated to AD 1054, long after this era. The churches of this era were in communion with each other, though they quarrelled. The splits that began in this era are different: the Church of the East after Ephesus, and the Oriental Orthodox churches after Chalcedon (both not yet checked here).

## Key beliefs and debates

### What did the council at Nicaea decide?

It issued a creed that says the Son is begotten, not made, and of one substance with the Father, and it condemns those who say there was a time when he was not (The Seven Ecumenical Councils, the Nicene Creed). A letter in the emperor's name to those who were absent urges them to keep Easter on one day. Popular accounts often credit Nicaea with more than that. The councils collection does not contain a list of Bible books from Nicaea, and the earliest lists in our sources come from Athanasius in 367 and from the African church, below.

### Which books are in the Bible?

Lists differed. Athanasius's list for the New Testament matches the one most Christians use today: four Gospels, Acts, seven catholic letters, fourteen letters of Paul including Hebrews, and Revelation. He says other books, including the Shepherd and the Teaching of the Apostles, are not in the canon but may be read to new believers. The African canon's list also has the same New Testament books, but its Old Testament includes Tobit, Judith, the Maccabees and Ezra, and it says nothing but canonical Scripture is to be read in church. Churches still differ on the Old Testament books to this day. How the lists came about is debated; see Further reading.

### Who was Arius, and what did he teach?

We know the Arian side mostly through the people who opposed it. The Nicene creed's condemnations, such as there was a time when he was not, show what the council took Arius's position to be. Historians disagree about how fairly the opponents reported it. See Further reading.

### One person in two natures

The council at Chalcedon says that the one Lord Jesus Christ is perfect in godhead and perfect in manhood, consubstantial with the Father and with us, and must be confessed in two natures, unconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably. Leo's Tome, read at the council, says each nature does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other. The definition was accepted by most churches and rejected by some, which is the origin of the Oriental Orthodox churches (not yet checked here).

### Grace and free will

Augustine's prayer, give what you command and command what you will, came to stand for his side in the dispute with Pelagius. The editors of his anti-Pelagian writings say Pelagius held that people could do whatever God commanded, and was provoked when the prayer was repeated in his presence. Augustine says it appears in his Confessions, written before the dispute. Christian traditions still differ on how to read the question.

### Did Constantine make the empire Christian?

The agreement of AD 313 gave Christians freedom, along with everyone else. This page does not yet check the laws of Theodosius that later favoured one kind of Christianity, so it does not claim that Christianity became the religion of the empire in this era. That claim is often overstated and needs a source before it appears here.

## Worship and community life

### Monks and the desert

Athanasius's Life says Antony was moved by hearing the Gospel passage in which Jesus tells a rich man to sell what he has and give to the poor. It was among the most widely read Christian books of the time, and Augustine says it was in his mind when he heard the voice in the garden. The monks became a new kind of Christian life, outside the cities.

### Bishops and emperors

Ambrose's letter to Theodosius shows a bishop telling an emperor that he could not offer the sacrifice in his presence until he repented. Whether the emperor then did penance, and how often bishops pressed rulers in this way, is not yet checked here.

### What was read in church

The African canon says that besides the canonical Scriptures nothing is to be read in church under the name of divine Scripture, and gives the list. Athanasius allows some books to be read to new believers that are not in the canon. The two together show that reading in church and the canon were not yet the same thing everywhere.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning on this page |
| --- | --- |
| Apocrypha | Books outside the lists some churches accept as Scripture, such as Tobit and Judith in Athanasius's and the African lists |
| Canon | The list of books accepted as Scripture |
| Consubstantial | Of one substance. The word used at Nicaea and Chalcedon |
| Council | A meeting of bishops to settle a question |
| Creed | A short statement of belief, such as the Nicene Creed |
| Donatists | North African Christians who would not accept bishops they thought had given up Scripture in persecution |
| Festal letter | A letter a bishop of Alexandria wrote each year before Easter |
| Monk | One who leaves ordinary life to live apart in prayer |
| Nature | In the Chalcedon definition, the divine or the human side of Christ |
| Pelagianism | The view, as Augustine describes it, that people can do all that God commands |
| Tome | Leo's letter to Flavian, bishop of Constantinople |
