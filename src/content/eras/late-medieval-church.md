## Overview

The eighth era ended when the Council of Constance elected Martin V in 1417 and so restored one pope. This era runs from there to Luther's Ninety-Five Theses in 1517. In those hundred years the Council of Basel claimed authority over the pope and made peace with the Hussites, the Greek and Latin churches proclaimed a union at Florence that the East did not accept, Constantinople fell to the Ottomans, and the popes of the Renaissance rebuilt Rome. Preachers such as Bernardino and Savonarola called for repentance, and an indulgence for the new St Peter's gave Luther his occasion.

## Key debates

### Should councils rule the church?

The Council of Basel claimed that it did, deposed Eugenius IV and elected a rival pope, but it ended by accepting Nicholas V in 1449. Schaff says Martin V and his successors feared councils and tried to keep them from meeting. The page does not go further than what the sources record.

### Did the union of Florence matter?

The decree of union was proclaimed in 1439 and read in Hagia Sophia in December 1452, but Schaff says it was rejected in Constantinople, which fell six months later, and that a Greek council rejected it formally in 1472.

### Was the Renaissance papacy a cause of reform?

Schaff, a Protestant, calls Julius II and Leo X worldly and avaricious, and says the rebuilding of St Peter's gave the occasion for the indulgences Luther attacked. The Catholic Encyclopedia records the general belief that Alexander VI bought his election. Whether the papacy caused the Reformation or only gave it a target, the sources read here do not settle.

## Worship and community life

### Devotion and the Brothers of the Common Life

Gerrit Groote of Deventer (1340 to 1384) and his followers formed the Brothers of the Common Life. Thomas à Kempis, the probable author of The Imitation of Christ, lived and copied books at Mount St Agnes near Zwolle. Bernardino of Siena preached in the Italian towns, where people burned their games and trinkets, and carried a banner with the monogram of Christ, IHS.

### Printing

Schaff dates the invention of the printing press to about 1450, and says Erasmus's Greek New Testament of 1516 was followed by more than thirty unauthorized reprints.
