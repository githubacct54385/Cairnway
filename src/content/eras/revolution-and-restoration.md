## Overview

The eleventh era ended when the French Revolution took the church's property and made its own constitution for the clergy. This era runs from that point to 1870. In those eighty years revolutionary France closed its churches and then made a concordat with Rome, two popes were taken prisoner, and the monarchies restored in 1815 bound themselves by a Christian alliance. The popes lost the Papal States piece by piece, and the last of them, Rome itself, on 20 September 1870, two months after the First Vatican Council defined papal infallibility. In England the Oxford Movement sought the Catholic roots of the Church of England, and some of its leaders went over to Rome. Protestants in Germany, Britain and America sought revival and union. In the East the Greek and Bulgarian churches broke from the patriarchate of Constantinople.

The page is arranged by theme. Events are tagged East or West. The Eastern churches are covered in less detail, as far as the Catholic Encyclopedia goes.

## The Revolution and the popes

Pius VI rejected the Civil Constitution of the Clergy in March 1791. In 1793 and 1794 the Convention replaced Sunday with the tenth-day rest of its new calendar, its representatives closed churches in the provinces, and Notre-Dame became a temple of Reason. When some came before the Convention dressed in vestments taken from a Paris church, its president said they had "wiped out eighteen centuries of error", the Catholic Encyclopedia records. Robespierre then had the Convention recognize a Supreme Being and the immortality of the soul. The French took Rome in 1798, and Pius VI died a prisoner at Valence in 1799.

## Napoleon and the Papal States

As First Consul, Bonaparte offered the pope a settlement of the religious question. The Concordat of 1801 restored public Catholic worship in France, under police rules set by the government, and gave the government the presentation of bishops. In 1802 the Organic Articles, which the pope protested against, were added to it. Pius VII came to Paris for the imperial coronation in 1804. In 1809 Napoleon annexed the Papal States and had the pope deported to Savona, and later to Fontainebleau. He was not free to return to Italy until 1814.

## Restoration

The Congress of Vienna gave the Papal States back to the pope, except Avignon, the Venaissin and a strip of Ferrara. Pius VII restored the Jesuits in 1814. The Holy Alliance of 1815 joined the rulers of Austria, Prussia and Russia. The Catholic Encyclopedia says the papacy and the bishops regarded it "with coldness", as an alliance "which took under its wings schism, heresy, and orthodoxy alike". Chateaubriand's *Génie du christianisme* of 1802 had, the encyclopedia says, soon made him famous.

## Protestant revival and union

Schaff dates a revival of evangelical Christianity in Germany from the Reformation jubilee of 1817, and describes the Prussian Union of the Lutheran and Reformed churches and the Old Lutheran secession that followed it. In America the Disciples of Christ, followers of Thomas and Alexander Campbell, formed an independent body in 1827, seeking a return to "original Christianity", Schaff says. In 1846 Protestants of many churches founded the Evangelical Alliance in London. Schaff says it took in Episcopalians, Lutherans, Presbyterians, Congregationalists, Baptists, Methodists and Moravians, while the High Church party kept away.

## Liberal Catholics

Lamennais founded *L'Avenir* in 1830 and, with Lacordaire and Montalembert, sought to defend the church by joining its cause to public liberties. Gregory XVI condemned some of its ideas in *Mirari vos* in 1832, and in 1834 Lamennais broke with the church.

## The Oxford Movement

John Keble's Assize sermon of 14 July 1833 warned of a nation turning from "the Apostolical Church". The *Tracts for the Times*, which ran from 1833 to 1841, gave the movement its other name, Tractarian. Newman's Tract 90, in 1841, brought on a collision with Protestant England, the Catholic Encyclopedia says, and he brought the series to an end at his bishop's request. Newman was received into the Roman Catholic Church at Littlemore on 9 October 1845, and others followed.

## Rome and Vatican I

Pius IX was elected in 1846 as the liberal candidate and began with an amnesty. After the revolution of 1848 he fled to Gaeta, and he returned in 1850 with French help. In 1854 he defined the Immaculate Conception. In 1860 the kingdom of Piedmont took all his lands except Rome. The *Syllabus* of 1864 listed eighty condemned propositions. The Vatican Council opened on 8 December 1869 and defined papal infallibility on 18 July 1870. The two sources give different counts for that last vote. Schaff says 535 fathers were present and all but two voted yes; the Catholic Encyclopedia says 435 assembled and 433 voted yes. On 20 September Italian troops entered Rome, and the council was adjourned in October.

## The Eastern churches

The Patriarch of Constantinople, Gregory V, was hanged in 1821 at the outbreak of the Greek War of Independence. In 1833, 36 bishops in Greece declared their church independent of Constantinople, and the patriarch recognized it in 1850. The Bulgarians had once had two patriarchates of their own, at Tirnovo, suppressed in 1393, and at Ochrida, suppressed in 1767, the Catholic Encyclopedia says. In 1870 the sultan granted them an exarchate, independent of the Greek patriarchate, whose exarch resided in Constantinople. The Catholic Encyclopedia says Patriarch Gregory VI refused even to accept Pius IX's invitation to the Vatican Council.

## Key debates

### The Concordat and the Organic Articles

The Catholic Encyclopedia says the Concordat "restored peace to the consciences of the French people", and that it "marked the final defeat of Gallicanism". It also says the Organic Articles, which later French governments kept in spite of the pope's protest, "in various ways infringed on the spirit of the concordat". The page has no source with another view, and so gives this one with its name.

### What was the Holy Alliance?

The Catholic Encyclopedia reports that when its text became known, many suspected "a league of the rulers and the churches, especially of the rulers and the papacy, against the nations and their freedom". The encyclopedia itself rejects this. It quotes Metternich: "It was solely an emanation of the pietistic feelings of the Emperor Alexander, and the application of the principles of Christianity to politics."

### Where was the Oxford Movement going?

The Catholic Encyclopedia gives two accounts. One, from the Royal Commission of 1906, sees the movement as recalling "the intimate and unbroken connection between the primitive Church and the Church of England". The other is Newman's own, in his *Lectures on Anglican Difficulties* of 1850: that its tendency was away from national churches altogether, towards Rome. Schaff, a Protestant, calls the Tractarians "Romanizing". Keble, by the encyclopedia's account, had "a great dislike of Rome".

### The Syllabus and Vatican I

Schaff calls the *Syllabus* "a strange mixture of truth and error", and "a declaration of war against modern civilization and the course of history for the last three hundred years". The Catholic Encyclopedia says each proposition must be read in the light of the document it was drawn from. It says the last proposition comes from an allocution of 1861, in which the pope said that if a system designed to de-Christianize the world is called progress and civilization, "he can never hold out the hand of peace to such a system".

On the council, the encyclopedia says the bishops of the minority who left Rome "did not oppose the dogma of papal infallibility itself, but were against its definition as inopportune". Schaff says that by leaving, "the opposition broke down by its own act of cowardice". He names Döllinger among the Old Catholic scholars who denied that the council was ecumenical, and the encyclopedia calls Döllinger, of Munich, the leading spirit of the movement in Germany hostile to the council.

## Worship and devotion

Pius IX extended the feast of the Sacred Heart to the whole world in 1856, the Catholic Encyclopedia says, and was devoted to Mary all his life. In revolutionary France the Convention set the rest of every tenth day against the Sunday rest, and under the Directory the Theophilanthropists tried to create "a spiritualist church without dogmas, miracles, priesthood or sacraments".
