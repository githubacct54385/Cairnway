## Overview

The eleventh era ended when the French Revolution took the church's lands and made its clergy into civil servants. This era
runs from that point to Vatican I in 1870. In those eighty years, the French state first persecuted the church and then made
terms with it, the restored monarchies tried to rebuild the old order, and the popes lost most of the lands they had ruled
for a thousand years. At the same time, a new religious feeling spread through Europe and America: Romantic, evangelical and
Catholic revivals that did not look back to the old order.

The page is arranged by theme. The East is covered in less detail, and its material is marked where it appears.

## The Revolution and the church's property

The Revolution went further against the church than the Civil Constitution of the Clergy had. Dechristianisation closed many
churches, and the state promoted a Cult of Reason and then a Cult of the Supreme Being. Pope Pius VI died in French captivity
at Valence in 1799. Details not yet checked.

## Napoleon and the Papal States

Napoleon's Concordat with Pius VII in 1801 restored public Catholic worship in France and gave the state a say in the
appointment of bishops. The Holy Roman Empire ended in 1806. In 1809 Napoleon annexed the Papal States and arrested Pius VII,
who was held in France until 1814. Details not yet checked.

## Restoration and reaction

The Congress of Vienna in 1815 restored many of the old monarchies, and the Holy Alliance of the Christian powers was meant to
hold them to Christian principles. Pius VII restored the Jesuits in 1814. The restored governments were often suspicious of
the church as well as of the revolutionaries. Details not yet checked.

## Romantic and evangelical revival

Chateaubriand's *The Genius of Christianity* (1802) made the beauty and history of Christianity a subject for educated readers.
Evangelical revival spread through England, Scotland and America, and the Second Great Awakening in the United States brought
large camp meetings and new churches. Missionary societies grew with it. Details not yet checked.

## The Oxford Movement

John Keble's Assize Sermon of 1833 began the Oxford Movement, which argued that the Church of England was a true part of the
catholic church. The *Tracts for the Times* followed. John Henry Newman was received into the Roman Catholic Church in 1845,
and others followed him. The movement changed Anglicanism for good. Details not yet checked.

## Liberal and critical answers

Félicité de Lamennais and his circle wanted the church to accept liberty and the press, and they were condemned for it.
Søren Kierkegaard attacked the Danish state church for its comfortable Christianity. Biblical and historical criticism began
to examine the texts of Scripture as human documents. Details not yet checked.

## Rome and Vatican I

Pius IX was elected in 1846 as a reforming pope and then turned against the liberal revolutions of 1848. He fled Rome in
November of that year and returned with French help. In 1854 he defined the Immaculate Conception. The *Syllabus of Errors*
(1864) condemned modern ideas, including the view that the pope should be reconciled with progress and liberalism. Vatican I
met from 1869 and defined papal infallibility on 18 July 1870. On 20 September 1870 Italian troops entered Rome, and the Papal
States came to an end. Details not yet checked.

## The Eastern churches

The Greek War of Independence began in 1821. The Ecumenical Patriarch Gregory V was hanged in Constantinople in the same year,
and his execution is one of the best known events of the war. The Ottoman sultan's Gülhane edict of 1839 began the Tanzimat
reforms, which gave new rights to non-Muslim communities. In 1870 a firman established the Bulgarian Exarchate, a church
separate from the Greek patriarchate. Details not yet checked. This section needs an Eastern specialist.

## Key debates

### Did the Revolution end the church's old place in the state?

The Concordat restored some of the church's public role, but on terms the state set. Historians debate how far the church's
position was changed for good, and how much the restoration was a return to the old order. The page does not yet give a view.

### Was the Oxford Movement a return to the past?

The Tractarians looked to the early church and the Fathers, but many of their ideas were new. Whether the movement was a
recovery or an innovation is debated. The page does not yet give a view.

### Did Vatican I answer the modern world?

Supporters saw the council as a defence of the faith against modern error. Critics, including some Catholic historians, saw it
as a turn away from the church's own traditions. The page does not yet give a view.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning on this page |
| --- | --- |
| Concordat | An agreement between the papacy and a state about the church's position in it |
| Dechristianisation | The attempt, in revolutionary France, to remove Christianity from public life |
| Infallibility | The Vatican I doctrine that the pope, speaking ex cathedra on faith and morals, cannot err |
| Tractarians | The writers of the *Tracts for the Times*, in the Oxford Movement |
| Tanzimat | The Ottoman reforms begun in 1839 |
