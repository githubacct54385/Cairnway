## Overview

The fourth era ended with Gregory the Great and the first English converts. This era runs from Gregory's successors to the Second Council of Nicaea in 787, which restored the veneration of images. In those years Arab armies took Syria, Jerusalem and Egypt from the Byzantine empire, the eastern church argued for decades over images, and the English church held its first councils.

The page is arranged by East and West, as before. Events are tagged East or West. The East in this era is the Byzantine empire, its councils and its emperors. The West is the English kingdoms, the Franks and the papacy in Rome.

## Key beliefs and debates

### Did Christ have one will or two?

The Sixth Council said Christ had two wills, human and divine, and condemned the view that he had one. The council also condemned Pope Honorius, who had died some years earlier, for supporting the one-will view. Whether Honorius's letters really taught the condemned view has long been argued over; the councils volume prints the council's sentence together with its editor's historical note on the question. This page reports the council's verdict and does not decide the question.

### Should Christians venerate images?

The Seventh Council said yes: images of Christ, of the Mother of God, of the apostles and of the saints may be venerated, though not worshipped as God. The emperors who had banned images before it argued that such veneration was idolatry. The council's own acts record both the confessions of the bishops who accepted the decree and the anathemas against those who did not. The councils volume's editor notes that the Frankish kingdom did not at first accept the council, and that Gibbon, who mocked its acts, had probably never read them.

### How should the English keep Easter?

Bede's account of Whitby shows the question was about more than a calendar. The two sides argued over the authority of Peter and the custom of the Scots. Oswy decided for Rome, and Bede says Colman then went back into Scotland with those who would not accept the Roman Easter and tonsure. Bede also says Abbess Hilda had been on Colman's side.

### What did the Arab conquests mean for the church?

The sources on this page show some of what changed: Jerusalem surrendered on terms its patriarch negotiated, and the councils volume's editor says that at the council of 787 the patriarchs of the conquered lands could not attend in person because of Muslim rule. How far the conquests changed Christian thought is a larger question that this page does not try to answer.

## Worship and community life

### Monks and the English church

Bede wrote about monasteries in Northumbria, and his account names the abbesses and monks there. The Synod of Whitby was held at the monastery of Abbess Hilda (Bede 3.25).

### Bishops and councils in the East

The Seventh Council shows bishops confessing the faith in turn, and anathematizing those who disagreed. It was called by the emperors Constantine VI and Irene, and the council of 754 that it overturned had been called by an emperor too.

## Islam: outside the sources used here

This page does not quote Muslim sources. Its account of the beginnings of Islam and of the conquests comes from Gibbon, who drew on Arabic historians, and it is limited to events and dates.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning on this page |
| --- | --- |
| Anathema | A formal curse by a council, cutting someone off from the church |
| Iconoclasm | The attack on the veneration of images |
| Monothelitism | The view that Christ had only one will, condemned by the Sixth Council |
| Synod | A church council, especially a local one (such as Whitby) |
| Tonsure | The shaving of the head that marked a monk or cleric; the English and the Scots differed on its form |
| Veneration | Honor given to images or saints, distinguished from the worship given to God |
