## Overview

The fourth era ended with Gregory the Great and the first English converts. This era runs from Gregory's successors to the Second Council of Nicaea in 787, which restored the veneration of images. In those years the church lost most of its eastern lands to the armies of a new faith, kept the faith of the Byzantine empire in a long argument over images, and built a church among the English and the Franks that would shape the West for centuries.

The page is arranged by East and West, as before. Events are tagged East or West. The East in this era is the Byzantine empire, its councils and its emperors. The West is the English kingdoms, the Franks and the papacy in Rome.

## Key beliefs and debates

### Did Christ have one will or two?

The Sixth Council said Christ had two wills, human and divine, and condemned the view that he had one. The council also condemned Pope Honorius, who had died some years earlier, for supporting the one-will view. Historians disagree about how far Honorius's writings support the charge. The page reports the council's view and the debate, but does not yet decide the question.

### Should Christians venerate images?

The Seventh Council said yes: images of Christ, of the Mother of God, of the apostles and of the saints may be venerated, though not worshipped as God. The emperors who had banned images before it argued that such veneration was idolatry. The council's own acts record both the confessions of the bishops who accepted the decree and the anathemas against those who did not. The dispute is an important one in the history of Christian art.

### How should the English keep Easter?

Bede's account of Whitby shows the question was about more than a calendar. The two sides argued over the authority of Peter and the custom of the Scots. Oswy decided for Rome, and Bede says Colman then returned home to Scotland. Scholars differ about how much the Whitby decision changed the English church, and this page does not yet settle that.

### Was the rise of Islam a turning point for the church?

The loss of Syria, Jerusalem and Egypt moved the centre of Christian life towards Constantinople and Rome. Historians debate how fast this happened and how far it changed Christian thought. This page does not yet make that argument, and its Islamic events are not yet checked.

## Worship and community life

### Monks and the English church

Bede wrote about monasteries in Northumbria, and his account names the abbesses and monks who kept the faith there. The Synod of Whitby was held at a monastery under Abbess Hilda. Monasteries were the centres of learning and of the church's life in the North.

### Bishops and councils in the East

The Seventh Council shows bishops confessing the faith in turn, and anathematising those who disagreed. Councils in this era were places of formal argument, but the acts also show how much depended on what the emperor wanted.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning on this page |
| --- | --- |
| Anathema | A formal curse by a council, cutting someone off from the church |
| Iconoclasm | The attack on the veneration of images |
| Monothelitism | The view that Christ had only one will, condemned by the Sixth Council |
| Synod | A church council, especially a local one (such as Whitby) |
| Tonsure | The shaving of the head that marked a monk or cleric; the English and the Scots differed on its form |
| Veneration | Honour given to images or saints, distinguished from the worship given to God |
