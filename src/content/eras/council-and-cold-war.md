## Overview

The thirteenth era ended with the Second World War in Europe. This era runs from that point to the fall of the Berlin Wall in
1989. In those forty-four years, the churches of Europe and the world were rebuilt and reshaped. The World Council of Churches
was founded, and the Catholic church held the Second Vatican Council, which changed its worship and its relations with other
churches and religions. In the East, communist states controlled the churches and imprisoned their leaders. In Latin America
and Africa the church grew fast, and liberation theology made the poor a central theme. Pope John Paul II, a Pole, helped to end
the Cold War in Europe. The page is arranged by theme, and the churches under communism are given their own section.

## Reconstruction and the new ecumenism

The World Council of Churches was founded at Amsterdam in 1948, bringing together many Protestant, Anglican and Orthodox
churches. Pius XII defined the Assumption of Mary as a dogma in 1950. Details not yet checked.

## The churches under communism

Communist governments took power across eastern Europe after the war. Cardinal Mindszenty of Hungary was arrested in December
1948. Cardinal Wyszyński of Poland was imprisoned from 1953 to 1956. The Hungarian uprising of 1956 was put down by Soviet forces,
and the churches' role in it is not yet checked. In the Soviet Union the Russian Orthodox Church was closely controlled by the
state, and in Romania and Bulgaria the churches were also controlled. Underground churches and groups kept faith alive in many
places. Details not yet checked. This section needs a specialist reviewer.

## The Second Vatican Council

John XXIII called the Council in 1959 and opened it on 11 October 1962. Its four main constitutions were *Dei Verbum* on
revelation, *Lumen Gentium* on the church, *Sacrosanctum Concilium* on the liturgy and *Gaudium et Spes* on the church in the
modern world. *Nostra Aetate* addressed the non-Christian religions, including Judaism. In December 1965 Paul VI and the
Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras lifted the mutual excommunications of 1054. Details not yet checked.

## The reception of the Council

The Council's changes, such as worship in the vernacular, were carried out in the years after 1965. *Humanae Vitae* (1968)
restated the church's teaching against artificial contraception, and it was received very differently across the church.
Paul VI led the church through the end of the Council, and John Paul I was pope for only a month in 1978. Details not yet
checked.

## A global church

The Latin American bishops met at Medellín in 1968 and applied the Council's teaching to the poverty of the continent. Liberation
theology grew from this, with figures such as Gustavo Gutiérrez. Oscar Romero, archbishop of San Salvador, spoke against the
violence of the state and was murdered while saying Mass in March 1980. Pentecostal and charismatic churches grew fast in Latin
America and Africa in the same years. Details not yet checked.

## John Paul II and the end of the Cold War

John Paul II was elected in October 1978. His visit to Poland in June 1979 drew very large crowds. Solidarity, the independent trade
union, was founded in Gdańsk in 1980 with the support of the church. The revolutions of 1989 brought down the communist governments
of central Europe, and the Berlin Wall fell on 9 November. The part the church played in these events is debated, and the page
does not yet give a view. Details not yet checked.

## Key debates

### Did the Council change the church, or only renew it?

Supporters saw the Council as a renewal of the church's own tradition. Critics, including some traditionalist Catholics, saw it as
a break with the past. The page does not yet give a view.

### What did the churches do under communism?

Some church leaders resisted the states, some cooperated, and many did both at different times. Historians debate how to weigh these
responses. The page does not yet give a view.

### Did the church help end the Cold War?

The role of John Paul II and of Solidarity in the revolutions of 1989 is widely discussed. How much of the change came from the church,
and how much from other causes, is debated. The page does not yet give a view.
