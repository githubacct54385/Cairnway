## Overview

The thirteenth era ended with the Second World War in Europe. This era runs from that point to the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989. In those forty-four years the World Council of Churches was founded, and the Catholic church held the Second Vatican Council, which changed its worship and its relations with other churches and religions. Rome and Constantinople lifted the excommunications of 1054. In the East, communist governments ruled most of central and eastern Europe. A Polish pope was elected in 1978, and in 1989 the Berlin Wall fell.

The page is arranged by theme. Events are tagged East or West. Most sources for this period are in copyright, and the readable ones are mostly Catholic documents. The Protestant and Orthodox churches, the churches under communism and the churches of Latin America and Africa are therefore covered in less detail than the draft planned.

## Reconstruction and the new ecumenism

The World Council of Churches was founded at Amsterdam on 23 August 1948. On 1 November 1950, in *Munificentissimus Deus*, Pius XII declared it "a divinely revealed dogma: that the Immaculate Mother of God, the ever Virgin Mary, having completed the course of her earthly life, was assumed body and soul into heavenly glory."

## The churches under communism

Communist governments ruled most of central and eastern Europe after the war. In Hungary, József Mindszenty had been archbishop of Esztergom since 1945 and a cardinal since 1946. The Hungarian revolution lasted from 23 October to 10 November 1956. In Poland, Stefan Wyszyński became archbishop of Gniezno and Warsaw in 1948 and a cardinal in 1953. At his beatification in 2021 Pope Francis said that he "was arrested and imprisoned". ## The Second Vatican Council

John XXIII was elected on 28 October 1958. In the bull *Humanae Salutis* (1961) he recalled that he had first announced an ecumenical council on 25 January 1959. The Council opened on 11 October 1962 and closed on 8 December 1965. Paul VI, elected on 21 June 1963 after John XXIII's death, promulgated its documents:

- *Sacrosanctum Concilium*, on the liturgy (4 December 1963). Because "the use of the mother tongue ... frequently may be of great advantage to the people, the limits of its employment may be extended."
- *Lumen Gentium*, on the church (21 November 1964).
- *Nostra Aetate*, on the non-Christian religions (28 October 1965). It says that "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures."
- *Dei Verbum*, on divine revelation (18 November 1965).
- *Gaudium et Spes*, on the church in the modern world (7 December 1965).

On 7 December 1965 a joint declaration of Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I was read in Rome and in Istanbul. It recalled the sentences of excommunication of 1054, and said that the two "commit these excommunications to oblivion". It added that this was "not sufficient to end both old and more recent differences" between the two churches.

## The reception of the Council

In *Pacem in Terris* (11 April 1963) John XXIII wrote on peace among nations. In *Humanae Vitae* (25 July 1968) Paul VI excluded "any action which either before, at the moment of, or after sexual intercourse, is specifically intended to prevent procreation—whether as an end or as a means."

## John Paul II and the end of the Cold War

The archbishop of Kraków was elected pope on 16 October 1978 as John Paul II. On 2 June 1979 he said Mass in Victory Square, Warsaw, and prayed: "Let your Spirit descend and renew the face of the earth, the face of this land." Solidarity, a trade union based in Gdańsk, was founded in September 1980. The Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989.

## Key debates

Three questions about this period are widely debated: whether the Council renewed the church's tradition or broke with it, how church leaders under communism should be judged, and how far the church helped to end the Cold War.
